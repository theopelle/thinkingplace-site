Epicureanism and Classical Greek Philosophy

Epicureanism · Classical Greek Philosophy

Epicurus taught in Athens a generation after Aristotle; both ask what makes a life go well, and they part on pleasure, the gods, and public life.

Where they agree

  • Goodness and a happy life go together. Epicurus says you cannot live pleasantly without living wisely, well, and justly; Aristotle calls the happy person one who lives well and does well.
  • Pleasure is tied to pain. Socrates, released from his chains, remarks how curiously pleasure follows pain; Epicurus says pleasure reaches its limit once pain is gone.
  • Friends matter. Aristotle says sorrow is lightened by the sympathy of friends; Epicurus calls friendship by far the most important means to a happy life.

Where they part ways

  • The goal. For Epicurus, pleasure, meaning freedom from pain and fear. For Aristotle, happiness is the active use of our abilities in line with virtue, over a whole life.
  • The world. Lucretius says fear comes from imagining gods at work where we don't know the causes, and the world is atoms and empty space. For Plato, the changing world is an imperfect copy of perfect, unchanging Forms.
  • Public life. Aristotle calls us social beings, and a citizen one who shares in governing. The Epicureans kept out of politics and lived among friends.
