Era

Renaissance Europe

14th–16th century

Keywords: humanism, Black Death, printing, Reformation, Florence, wars of religion

After the plague, Europe went back to the books of Greece and Rome to find a better way to live, and found itself questioning the Church, the prince, and its own customs.

How people lived

The Black Death reached Europe in 1347. In a few years it killed perhaps 30 to 60 per cent of the population, by different estimates. Workers became scarce and wages rose. The old order of lords and peasants began to loosen.

Italy was a patchwork of rich, rival city-states. Florence, where the Renaissance began, was run by merchant families such as the Medici, who paid artists like Leonardo and Michelangelo. From about 1440, printing spread from one shop in Mainz to some 270 cities. By 1500, more than 20 million books had been printed.

What they believed

Nearly everyone was Christian, and the Church shaped every part of life. The humanists did not reject faith. They wanted to go back to the sources: ancient texts read in their original languages, for lessons in how to live and speak well.

Petrarch (1304–1374) hunted through old libraries for lost Latin works by Cicero and Seneca. Erasmus, the most printed author of the early 1500s, published the New Testament in Greek in 1516. He wanted the Church reformed from within and stayed Catholic all his life.

Who counted, and who was left out

When the plague came, Jews were falsely accused of poisoning wells. In 1349 about 2,000 were murdered in Strasbourg alone, and by 1351 hundreds of Jewish communities had been destroyed. Many fled east to Poland.

Women rarely appear in the record of the age. A few broke through, like the painter Sofonisba Anguissola. After 1492, Europeans also met the peoples of the Americas for the first time.

Ahead of their time

Europeans took it for granted that their own world was the civilised one. Montaigne wrote about the Tupinambá of Brazil, who ate their dead enemies, and compared their customs with the cruelty of Europe in his own day. He judged both by the same standard.

Writers on government usually told princes how a good ruler should behave. Machiavelli lost his post when the Medici returned to Florence in 1512, and was imprisoned and tortured. In 1513 he drafted The Prince, which looked instead at how rulers really gain and keep power.

What still echoes today

In 1517 Luther challenged the Pope, and Europe split. In France, wars between Catholics and Protestants ran from 1562 to 1598 and cost perhaps 2 to 4 million lives. In 1572, thousands of Protestants were killed in the St Bartholomew's Day massacre.

Montaigne lived through all of it. He withdrew to his tower in 1571 to write his Essays, then served as mayor of Bordeaux, respected by both sides. His habit of doubting his own certainty is the era's most useful gift.
