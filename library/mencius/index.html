Thinker

Mencius

Zoucheng, China · about 371 – 289 BCE · Confucianism

Keywords: human nature, righteousness, the people first

Who he was

Mencius, born Meng Ke, came from the small state of Zou, in eastern China, about a century after Confucius. He lost his father young, and his mother, who raised him, became a model of wise parenting in Chinese tradition. He is said to have studied in the school of Confucius's grandson, Zisi.

Like Confucius, he spent years travelling from court to court, advising rulers during the violent Warring States period. He served for a time as an official and scholar in the state of Qi, and left to mourn his mother for three years. Disappointed that rulers ignored his advice, he finally withdrew from public life.

What he cared about

The book named after him, the Mencius, records his conversations with rulers and students. His central claim: human nature is good.

  • Anyone who sees a child about to fall into a well feels alarm at once, without thinking of reward. That is the seed of goodness in all of us.
  • Four beginnings: compassion, shame, respect, and a sense of right and wrong, which we must grow like plants.
  • Shame matters: a person without it cannot become good.
  • Rulers serve the people: a state that governs humanely will flourish.

What might surprise you

The gentle optimist was also a political radical for his time. He argued that people may overthrow, or even kill, a ruler who ignores their needs and rules harshly, because such a ruler is no longer a true ruler.

His view of human nature set him against another Confucian, Xunzi, who thought people are born bad. Centuries later, his book became one of the Four Books at the core of Confucian education, and he is honoured as the "Second Sage".

Where to start reading

The Mencius, Book II, Part I, with the child at the well. James Legge's 1895 translation is free online.
