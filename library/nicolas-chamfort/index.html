Thinker

Nicolas Chamfort

Clermont-Ferrand, France · about 1741 – 1794 · Humanists and Essayists

Keywords: Maxims and Thoughts, moralists

Who he was

Chamfort was born around 1741 (sources differ on the date) in Clermont-Ferrand, and raised by a grocer. Local tradition said he was the secret child of a noblewoman and a clergyman. At nine he went to Paris on a scholarship, and later lived for years on the hospitality of people who enjoyed his conversation.

Success came slowly, then all at once. His plays were performed before Louis XVI and Marie Antoinette, he received royal pensions, and in 1781 he was elected to the Académie française. But he disliked court life and kept stepping away from it.

When the Revolution came, he threw himself into it. He was among the first to enter the Bastille and served as secretary of the Jacobin club. Then his jokes turned on the new rulers, and during the Terror he was jailed. Threatened with arrest again, he tried to take his own life, and he died of his wounds months later, in April 1794.

What he cared about

Chamfort left notes of maxims and anecdotes, published after his death as Maxims and Thoughts. His themes:

  • Laughter: the most wasted day is the one without it.
  • Society's masks: he watched the salons and the court closely and showed what lay behind the manners.
  • Freedom: he hated servility, whether to a king or to a revolution.
  • Self-made knowledge: he joked that he had forgotten what he was taught and only knew what he had guessed.
  • Books over people: the great dead, he said, make better company than the living.

What might surprise you

His wit cost him his life. A republican through and through, he could not stop mocking the revolutionary government, and it was his sarcasm, not royalism, that made him a suspect.

His maxims are often set beside La Rochefoucauld's. They are less polished but more violent, written in a time of storm. John Stuart Mill praised them highly.

Where to start reading

Maxims and Thoughts: short and sharp, best read a few at a time. Free in French on Project Gutenberg; the app uses its own translations.
