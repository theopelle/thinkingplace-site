Theme

Desire and simplicity

Keywords: craving, wanting less, contentment, non-attachment, possessions, frugality

The question in one line

Would we be happier wanting less?

How the traditions see it

Epicurus (Epicureanism). Epicurus sorted desires into three kinds: natural and necessary, like drinking when thirsty; natural but not necessary, like costly food; and neither, like statues in your honour. The first are easy to satisfy; the last have no limit. Nature's wealth, he said, is small and easy to get.

Marcus Aurelius, Epictetus, and Seneca (Stoicism). Marcus Aurelius advised doing few things if you want peace of mind. Epictetus put it more bluntly: don't admire your clothes, and you won't be angry with the thief. Seneca noted that we fear everything as if we were mortal, and want everything as if we would live forever.

The Bhagavad Gita and the Dhammapada (Indian Philosophy, Buddhist Philosophy). The Gita traces a chain: dwelling on things breeds attachment, attachment breeds desire, and desire breeds anger. Its answer is to act without clinging to the fruits of action. The Dhammapada says there is no fire like passion.

Laozi and Thoreau (Taoism, Humanists and Essayists). For Laozi, there is no greater calamity than never being content. Many centuries later, Thoreau went to live by Walden Pond to keep only the essentials of life. His advice: "Simplify, simplify."

Where they agree, and where they don't

Most agree that endless wanting makes us unhappy, and that it is better to change our desires than the world. Not everyone wants to drop desire, though. Émilie du Châtelet wrote that we are happy only through tastes and passions that are satisfied, and Simone Weil saw desire as what leads the mind.
