Theme

Death and mortality

Keywords: dying, grief, memento mori, afterlife, fear of death

The question in one line

Should we think about death, and can we stop fearing it?

How the traditions see it

Epicurus and Lucretius (Epicureanism). Epicurus argued that death is nothing to us: where death is, we are not, and where we are, death is not. Lucretius asked why we should not leave life like a guest leaving a banquet, full and content. On the day he died, in great pain, Epicurus wrote to a friend that the memory of their conversations outweighed it all.

Marcus Aurelius and Montaigne (Stoicism, Humanists and Essayists). Marcus Aurelius reminded himself not to act as if he had ten thousand years. Montaigne took an old saying for an essay title: to study philosophy is to learn to die. Whoever has learned to die, he said, has unlearned how to be a slave.

Zhuangzi (Taoism). When Zhuangzi's wife died, a friend found him beating on a bowl and singing. He had grieved like anyone, he explained, then saw her death as one more change, like spring turning to summer, autumn, and winter. Tagore says something close: death belongs to life as birth does.

Omar Khayyam and Kenkō (Persian and Sufi Poetry, Japanese Aesthetics). For Khayyam, death is a door with no key and a veil no one sees past, so we should make the most of the days we have. Kenkō notes that death does not wait its turn like the seasons; it presses on us from behind.

Where they agree, and where they don't

Most of them say that facing death honestly makes life clearer and fear smaller. They split on how much to think about it. Montaigne and the Stoics advise keeping it in mind; Spinoza answered that a free person thinks of death least of all, and that wisdom is a meditation on life.
