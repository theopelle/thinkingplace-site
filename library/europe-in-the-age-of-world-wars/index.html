Era

Europe in the age of world wars

about 1914–1945

Keywords: First World War, Second World War, Holocaust, 20th century, world wars

Thirty years of total war, mass murder, and broken faith in progress, when philosophers asked what a human life is worth and what we owe one another.

Why only one thinker here

This era produced many of the thinkers people know best today, among them Sartre, Simone de Beauvoir, and Camus. Their works are still protected by copyright, so the app cannot yet share their words the way it shares older texts. Simone Weil stands here alone for that reason, not because she was the only voice of her time. We will add others as their works enter the public domain.

How people lived

The First World War, from 1914 to 1918, killed somewhere between 15 and 22 million people, soldiers and civilians. France alone lost about 1.7 million, more than 4 per cent of its population.

The peace did not last. The Second World War killed somewhere between 60 and 85 million people, about 3 per cent of everyone alive in 1940.

What they believed

After the slaughter of 1914–1918, the belief that the world was steadily getting better was hard to hold. People looked for meaning in politics, in faith, or in neither.

Simone Weil, born in Paris in 1909 to a secular Jewish family, tried more than one of these paths. She studied philosophy at the École Normale Supérieure, taught, and was a pacifist until Hitler's seizure of Prague in 1939 changed her mind.

Who counted, and who was left out

Between 1941 and 1945, Nazi Germany and its allies murdered around six million Jews, about two-thirds of the Jews of Europe. Millions of others were also persecuted and killed. In 1942 Weil left France with her family for the United States, then went to London to work for the Free French.

Women in France did not gain the right to vote until 1944, and first voted in 1945. Until 1965, a married Frenchwoman needed her husband's consent to take a job.

Ahead of their time

Few philosophers had seen factory work from the inside. In 1934, Weil took a year off teaching to work, unknown, on factory assembly lines, including at Renault, to learn what that labour does to a person. In 1936 she went to Spain to join an anarchist unit in the civil war, until an accident forced her home.

Since the French Revolution, European politics had been built on the language of rights. Weil argued that obligations come first: a right means something only when others do their duty to respect it. In 1943, as de Gaulle's Free French drafted a new declaration of the rights of man, she wrote a draft statement of human obligations instead, and set out her view in The Need for Roots.

What still echoes today

Weil died in England in 1943, aged 34, of heart failure, while ill with tuberculosis and eating no more than she believed people in occupied France were given. Almost all her major works were published only after her death. Her questions about attention, the dignity of work, and the need for roots are still ours.

The questions of this era sit under much of today's thinking about human rights, about work, and about how ordinary people can be drawn into great evil.
