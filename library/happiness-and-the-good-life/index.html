Theme

Happiness and the good life

Keywords: pleasure, eudaimonia, flourishing, contentment, wellbeing, joy

The question in one line

What does a good life need, and is happiness the point?

How the traditions see it

Aristotle (Classical Greek Philosophy). Happiness, for Aristotle, is the one thing we want for its own sake. His word, eudaimonia, means living well, or flourishing, not a passing mood. It is judged over a whole life: one swallow does not make a spring, and one good day does not make a happy life.

Epicurus (Epicureanism). Epicurus made pleasure the goal, but not the pleasure of parties and luxury. He meant freedom from pain in the body and fear in the mind. Plain food, once hunger is gone, gives as much pleasure as a feast, and friends are the greatest help of all.

Confucius and the Dhammapada (Confucianism, Buddhist Philosophy). Both find happiness in contentment. Confucius said he could still find joy with coarse rice, water, and his bent arm for a pillow. For the Dhammapada, health is the greatest gift and contentment the best riches.

Mill and Kant (Enlightenment Ethics). Mill believed happiness is the goal, but some pleasures are higher than others: better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied. He also warned that happiness comes best on the side, while aiming at something else. Kant answered that morality is not about how to be happy, but how to become worthy of happiness.

Where they agree, and where they don't

Most agree that happiness is not pleasure piled up, and that wanting less helps. They disagree on whether happiness is the aim of life. For Aristotle, Epicurus, and Mill it is, in different forms; for Kant, doing right comes first, whatever it costs.
