Karma and dharma

Keywords: dhamma, svadharma, yoga, rebirth, intention

The question in one line

Do our actions shape who we become, and is there a right path for each of us?

How the traditions see it

The Upanishads (Indian Philosophy). Karma simply means "action". In the older Vedic religion, it meant ritual acts that could earn a place in heaven. The Upanishads made it inward. In one of them, a person is made of desires: as we desire, so we will, and as we will, so we act. What we do, in turn, shapes what we become, in this life and the next.

The Bhagavad Gita (Indian Philosophy). Dharma comes from a root meaning "to hold" or "to support": the order that holds life together, and the right way to live within it. The Gita adds svadharma, your own dharma, and says it is better to follow your own path, even badly, than someone else's well. Its answer to karma is to act fully, but without craving the results. Yoga, in the Gita, is that discipline of action.

The Buddhist texts (Buddhist Philosophy). The Buddha moved karma from the act to the intention behind it. It is not fate: past actions shape what happens to us, but not how we respond. In Buddhism, Dhamma (the Pali form of dharma) also means something else: the Buddha's teaching itself, and the truth it points to. When the Dhammapada speaks of "the law", this is what it means.

Where they agree, and where they don't

Both traditions agree that actions have consequences that reach beyond the moment, and that what we do slowly makes us who we are. They part on the self: the Upanishads have a lasting self that carries its karma, while Buddhism has no permanent self at all. There is also an uncomfortable side. In Hindu law, each person's own dharma was tied to their class and station in life. Early Buddhist texts pushed back: what makes a person noble or low is their deeds, not their birth.
