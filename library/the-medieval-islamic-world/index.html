Era

The medieval Islamic world

11th–14th century

Keywords: Baghdad, Persia, Seljuks, Mongols, Islamic Golden Age, Sufis

A world of great cities, schools, and libraries, broken by the Mongol invasions, where scholars weighed reason against faith and poets turned the love of God into some of the most loved verse ever written.

How people lived

By the 11th century, Baghdad had been a centre of learning for centuries. Scholars there had translated Plato, Aristotle, Galen, and Ptolemy from Greek into Arabic. Paper, which came from China in the 8th century, made books cheaper and easier to spread.

Power had passed to Turkish rulers, the Seljuks. Their vizier Nizam al-Mulk founded a network of colleges, the first in Baghdad in 1065. Omar Khayyam, born in Nishapur in 1048, worked for the Seljuk sultan, building an observatory and reforming the Persian calendar. He was also a serious mathematician, who developed the first general theory of cubic equations.

What they believed

Islam shaped law, learning, and daily life. Within it, two paths met and sometimes clashed: the jurists, who studied the law, and the Sufis, who sought closeness to God through love, prayer, and the remembrance of God.

From the 12th century, Sufi brotherhoods formed and spread across the Muslim world. Persian poets such as Rumi, Sa'di, and Hafiz gave this inner search its most famous voice.

Who counted, and who was left out

The colleges were open only to men. Women from leading city families were often taught at home, and many earned certificates in religious studies, calligraphy, or poetry. Some women worked as physicians in hospitals.

Slavery was part of life, including in the army. Mamluks were boys bought, often from Turkic lands, converted, and trained as soldiers. Freed as adults, they formed a ruling class, and from 1250 they ruled Egypt and Syria themselves.

Ahead of their time

The jurists, who studied the law, and the Sufis, who sought God through love, were often at odds. Al-Ghazali, a professor at the great college in Baghdad, refused to choose. He brought law and Sufi practice together, and helped Sufism spread.

To many readers, the language of wine and drunkenness had no place in religion. Sufi poets such as Hafiz used it anyway, as an image of the love of God. Some critics doubted whether this was still Islam.

What still echoes today

In 1219 the Mongols invaded. They destroyed great cities such as Merv, Nishapur, and Herat. In 1258 they took Baghdad, killed the caliph, and massacred its people, an event often seen as the end of the Golden Age of Islamic learning.

Yet much of the poetry was written in or after these years of loss. Rumi's and Hafiz's verses are still read, quoted, and sung today, far beyond the Muslim world.
