Thinker

Blaise Pascal

Clermont (now Clermont-Ferrand, France) · 1623 – 1662 · Existential Thought

Keywords: Pensées, the heart's reasons, diversion

Who he was

Pascal was born in Clermont, today's Clermont-Ferrand, and lost his mother at three. His father, a tax official, taught him at home. He was a child prodigy: at 16 he wrote an important work on geometry, and at 19 he began building one of the first mechanical calculators.

He went on to shape the theory of probability, in letters with the mathematician Fermat, and to clarify how air pressure and the vacuum work; the unit of pressure is named after him. He was often ill, and died in Paris at 39.

What he cared about

On the night of 23 November 1654, Pascal had an intense religious experience and wrote a short note about it to himself. After that, he turned from science to faith and the human condition. His questions:

  • Our place in the universe: we are tiny and fragile, yet we can think, and that is our dignity.
  • Distraction: we keep busy so that we never have to sit alone with ourselves.
  • The limits of reason: the heart knows things that reason cannot prove.
  • The wager: a famous argument that, faced with uncertainty, believing in God is the better bet.

What might surprise you

His best-known book is not really a book. The Pensées ("Thoughts") are scraps of paper for a defence of Christianity that he never finished, edited by others and published in 1670, eight years after his death. Today they count among the masterpieces of French prose.

He also wrote one of the most quoted apologies in literature. In his Provincial Letters, he excused a long letter by saying he had not had time to write a shorter one.

Where to start reading

The Pensées: short fragments, some just a sentence, others a page. C. Kegan Paul's 1885 translation is free on Project Gutenberg.
