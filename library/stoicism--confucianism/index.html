Two paths

Stoicism and Confucianism

Stoicism · Confucianism

Two ethics of daily self-examination, one Roman and one Chinese; they part on where our duties come from, and on how to answer a wrong.

Where they agree

  • Look at yourself every day. A disciple of Confucius asks himself three questions each day; Seneca looked back each evening at how he had spent his day.
  • Deeds before words. Confucius says the superior person acts first and speaks afterwards; Marcus Aurelius tells himself to stop talking about what a good person is, and to be one.
  • A clear conscience is calm. Confucius asks what there is to fear when looking inside finds nothing wrong; for the Stoics, only our own judgments and actions are truly ours to worry about.

Where they part ways

  • Where duty comes from. The Stoics root it in a reason all humans share: we are made for co-operation, like hands and feet. Confucius roots it in the family and in ritual: respect for parents is where a good society begins.
  • Answering a wrong. Confucius says repay injury with justice, and kindness with kindness. Epictetus asks us to take a brother's wrong by its other handle: he is still our brother.
