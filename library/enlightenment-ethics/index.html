Path

Enlightenment Ethics

Keywords: Enlightenment, reason, rights, utilitarianism, categorical imperative, social contract

The age that put reason, evidence, and freedom at the centre of how we live and govern ourselves.

Where and when

The Enlightenment grew out of the scientific revolution of Galileo and Newton. It took shape in Western Europe in the 17th century; one common starting point is Descartes' Discourse on Method (1637).

It peaked in the 18th century, in salons, coffeehouses, academies, and a booming world of print. Its great project was the French Encyclopédie (1751–1772), edited by Diderot and d'Alembert. Historians usually end the period with the French Revolution in 1789, or at the latest with Kant's death in 1804. Our path stretches a little further, to Mill in the 1860s.

Key ideas

  • Think for yourself: test beliefs by reason and evidence, not by tradition alone.
  • Why we need government (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau): power rests on an agreement among the people, not on birth.
  • Freedom and tolerance: of religion, of speech, of how you live.
  • Morality from feeling or from duty: Hume and Adam Smith start from sympathy; Kant from duty; Mill from the greatest happiness.
  • Women's minds (Wollstonecraft): women are rational beings and deserve the same education.

Next to its neighbours

The Humanists before them looked back to ancient wisdom; the Enlightenment looked forward, to progress. Its ideals also had clear limits. Many European colonies ran on slave labour, and even Locke, who called freedom a natural right, allowed slavery for captives taken in a just war. In 1791, enslaved people in the French colony of Saint-Domingue rose up, in what became the Haitian Revolution.
