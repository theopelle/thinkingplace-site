Taoism and Japanese Aesthetics

Taoism · Japanese Aesthetics

Okakura Kakuzō explains Japanese beauty partly through Taoism; the Japanese writers take Laozi's emptiness and simplicity and turn them into a way of looking.

Where they agree

  • Emptiness is useful. Laozi points to the hollow of a pot and the space inside a room; Okakura applies the image to a water pitcher and the almost empty tea room.
  • Leave it unfinished. Laozi says the partial becomes complete; Okakura finds true beauty in completing the incomplete in the mind, and Kenkō would rather leave a little imperfection.
  • Want little. Frugality is one of Laozi's three treasures; Chōmei lived in a ten-foot-square hut.

Where they part ways

  • How passing feels. Zhuangzi, though moved by his wife's death, comes to see it as one phase giving way to the next, like the seasons. For Kenkō, change is what touches us, with a gentle sadness he values.
  • Refinement. Laozi distrusts fine words and polish. The Japanese writers made an art of taste: Sei Shōnagon's lists of what is and isn't beautiful, the careful forms of the tea ceremony.
