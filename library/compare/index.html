Compare two paths

Where two paths agree, and where they part ways, with a few of their thoughts side by side.

Pick two

Pairs to read

  • Stoicism and EpicureanismTwo schools from the same city and the same century, both after a calm mind: the Stoics through virtue, the Epicureans through pleasure, rightly understood.
  • Epicureanism and Japanese AestheticsMore than thirteen centuries and half the world apart, both find the good life in simple things, but they feel very differently about the fact that everything passes.
  • Stoicism and Buddhist PhilosophyBoth trace our suffering to the mind rather than to events, and both treat philosophy as daily training; they part on the self and on where the training leads.
  • Stoicism and TaoismBoth say live in agreement with nature and stop fighting what you cannot change; but for the Stoics nature means reason and duty, and for the Taoists it means yielding and doing less.
  • Epicureanism and Buddhist PhilosophyBoth say we suffer from wanting too much, and both aim at a calm mind; they part on pleasure, and on what happens when a life ends.
  • Epicureanism and Enlightenment EthicsAbout two thousand years apart, Epicurus and Mill both put happiness at the centre of ethics, and both had to defend it against the charge of mere indulgence; they part on which pleasures count.
  • Persian and Sufi Poetry and EpicureanismKhayyam and Epicurus both say enjoy today, with little; but Epicurus seeks a mind free of longing, while the Persian poets live with it, and for the Sufis it leads towards the divine.
  • Classical Greek Philosophy and ConfucianismConfucius died about the time Socrates was born; on opposite sides of the world, both built an ethics of character, and they part on where good character is formed.
  • Classical Greek Philosophy and Enlightenment EthicsAristotle asks what kind of person to be; the Enlightenment adds two more questions, what is my duty and what brings the most happiness: between them, three of the main ways people still judge right and wrong.
  • Confucianism and TaoismChina's classic pair, grown side by side: the Confucians build a good life through learning, ritual, and duty; the Taoists trust the natural way and doing less.
  • Taoism and Japanese AestheticsOkakura Kakuzō explains Japanese beauty partly through Taoism; the Japanese writers take Laozi's emptiness and simplicity and turn them into a way of looking.
  • Buddhist Philosophy and Japanese AestheticsBoth start from the same fact, that everything passes, and two of the Japanese writers, Chōmei and Kenkō, became Buddhist monks; but Buddhism teaches us to stop clinging, while they linger over passing things as beauty.
  • Buddhist Philosophy and Existential ThoughtBoth begin with suffering rather than with theory; the Buddhist texts offer a way out of it, while these writers mostly stay with it, and some learn to say yes to it.
  • Humanists and Essayists and Japanese AestheticsMontaigne and Kenkō, about 250 years and half the world apart, both left public life to write short, wandering pieces about themselves, death, and small things.
  • Indian Philosophy and Buddhist PhilosophyBorn in the same world and sharing karma and rebirth, the two part on the self: the Upanishads look for a deep, lasting self, and Buddhism says there is none.
  • Indian Philosophy and StoicismDo your work and let go of the results: the Bhagavad Gita and the Stoics come strikingly close here, for different reasons.
  • Indian Philosophy and Existential ThoughtSchopenhauer called the Upanishads the solace of his life; beyond him, the two paths ask the same questions about freedom and meaning, and answer them very differently.
  • Indian Philosophy and Persian and Sufi PoetryBoth see love and devotion as a road to the divine, and both ask us to master the lower self; they part on whether the end of the road is knowing or longing.
  • Indian Philosophy and Classical Greek PhilosophyBoth pictured the self as the driver of a chariot, and both asked whether we choose the good or the pleasant; they part on what the self finally is.
  • Stoicism and Enlightenment EthicsSeveral Enlightenment thinkers came close to the Stoics on mastering our desires through reason; they part on whether the passions are to be corrected or are the very force that moves us.
  • Stoicism and Classical Greek PhilosophyThe Stoics grew out of the Greek world after Aristotle and kept Socrates as an example; they agree that virtue is the heart of a good life, and part on whether it is enough.
  • Stoicism and ConfucianismTwo ethics of daily self-examination, one Roman and one Chinese; they part on where our duties come from, and on how to answer a wrong.
  • Taoism and Buddhist PhilosophyBoth say still the mind, let go, and stop clinging; the Taoists follow the natural way of things, the Buddhist texts a path out of suffering.
  • Epicureanism and Classical Greek PhilosophyEpicurus taught in Athens a generation after Aristotle; both ask what makes a life go well, and they part on pleasure, the gods, and public life.
  • Persian and Sufi Poetry and StoicismBoth teach patience with fortune, which gives and takes away; the Stoics meet it with reason, the Sufi poets with love.
  • Persian and Sufi Poetry and Buddhist PhilosophyBoth see our own cravings as the real enemy and ask for a heart wiped clean; they part on where that leads: for the Buddhist texts to calm, for the Sufi poets to love.
  • Indian Philosophy and TaoismBoth teach a way of acting without strain: the Gita and Vivekananda act fully but let go of the results, the Taoists act without forcing at all.
  • Epicureanism and TaoismBoth praise a simple life with few desires and distrust fame and wealth; they part on company and on knowledge.
  • Confucianism and Enlightenment EthicsBoth build ethics on how we treat one another and how power should behave; Confucius starts from roles, ritual, and family, the Enlightenment from free individuals and their agreement.
  • Indian Philosophy and ConfucianismBoth say our duties depend on our place in life, and both prize self-mastery and example; they part on where those duties finally lead.
  • Indian Philosophy and Enlightenment EthicsVivekananda and Kant both say do good because it is good, not for a reward; they part on what grounds that duty, and on what freedom means.
