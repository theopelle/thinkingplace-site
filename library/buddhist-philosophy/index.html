Buddhist Philosophy

Keywords: Buddha, nirvana, not-self, mindfulness, Dhamma, impermanence

The Buddha's teachings read as a practical philosophy: an ethics and a training of the mind to end suffering.

Where and when

The Buddha, born Gautama in Lumbini, in today's Nepal, taught in northern India, most likely in the 5th century BCE. His teaching began as one of several movements of wandering seekers, then spread across Asia along the Silk Road, and reached the West in the 19th century.

Today it is both a religion, followed by hundreds of millions of people, and a philosophy studied by many who do not practise it. The app reads it mainly as philosophy, through two texts: the Dhammapada, a collection of 423 short verses in 26 chapters, and The Questions of King Milinda, a dialogue between a Buddhist monk and a Greek king of north-west India.

Key ideas

  • *Life is marked by dukkha:* suffering, or the unease of relying on things that do not last.
  • Everything changes, yet we crave and cling to what cannot stay.
  • No fixed self: there is no permanent, unchanging soul behind our thoughts and feelings.
  • Actions have consequences (karma): what we do shapes who we become.
  • The Middle Way between harsh self-denial and indulgence, through ethics, meditation, and wisdom.

Who we read

  • The Dhammapadasayings of the Buddha, 5th century BCE
  • The Questions of King Milindaabout 100 BCE – 200 CE

Next to its neighbours

Buddhism grew in the same soil as Indian Philosophy and shares ideas such as karma and rebirth. But it parts ways on the self: where the Upanishads look for a deep, lasting self, Buddhism denies there is one. Its focus on calming the mind also echoes the Stoics and Epicureans, a few centuries later and a long way west.
