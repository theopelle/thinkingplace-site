Stoicism and Classical Greek Philosophy

Stoicism · Classical Greek Philosophy

The Stoics grew out of the Greek world after Aristotle and kept Socrates as an example; they agree that virtue is the heart of a good life, and part on whether it is enough.

Where they agree

  • Never return a wrong. Socrates says we must not repay evil with evil; Marcus Aurelius, that the best revenge is not to become like the wrongdoer.
  • Wrongdoing is worse than death. Socrates says the hard thing is not to avoid death but to avoid wrongdoing; Epictetus points to Socrates to show that death is not terrible in itself.
  • Steady in good and bad times. Aristotle's great-souled person neither rejoices too much in prosperity nor grieves too much in adversity; Seneca says the invulnerable is not what is never struck, but what is never wounded.

Where they part ways

  • Is virtue enough? For the Stoics, virtue is the only good. For Aristotle, happiness is a whole life lived well, and no one would choose to live without friends, even with every other good thing.
  • Anger. Aristotle wants anger at the right person, to the right degree, at the right time. For the Stoics, strong emotions like anger come from false judgments, to be corrected rather than measured.
