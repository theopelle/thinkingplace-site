Era

Ancient Greece

6th–3rd century BCE

Keywords: Athens, city-states, classical antiquity, Hellenistic, Greeks

Small, proud cities where a few free men argued about everything, and most people had no say at all.

How people lived

Greece was not one country but hundreds of city-states, each with its own laws, gods, and army. Athens, the best known, became a democracy around 508 BCE: citizens voted directly in an assembly. It was also a city at war for much of this time. In 404 BCE it lost a long war against Sparta, and a harsh regime of thirty oligarchs briefly took power.

After Alexander the Great died in 323 BCE, the cities lost their independence to large kingdoms. Athens was defeated in 322 BCE, and Macedonian soldiers held its port. This is the world Epicurus taught in, from around 306 BCE: a city that could no longer steer its own fate.

What they believed

Greek religion had no holy book, no single priesthood, and no creed. It was something you did more than something you believed: sacrifices at altars, libations of wine, and festivals, which filled some 140 days of the Athenian year. People consulted oracles, above all the priestess of Apollo at Delphi, and most imagined the dead as spirits in an underworld.

Religion was bound up with the city itself. That is why, in 399 BCE, Socrates was charged with failing to honour the city's gods and with corrupting the young. A jury of about 500 citizens found him guilty, 280 to 221, and sentenced him to death.

Who counted, and who was left out

Only adult male citizens could vote. Athenian women had no legal standing of their own: a father, then a husband, acted for them, and girls often married around 14, to men twice their age. Religious festivals were almost the only public life open to them. Foreign residents, called metics, paid a special tax and could neither vote nor own land.

And Athens ran on slavery. There may have been some 80,000 slaves in the city in the 6th and 5th centuries BCE, and most free families owned at least one. The worst work was in the silver mines. Few Greeks questioned this. Aristotle called slaves "living tools"; one of the rare dissenters, the orator Alcidamas, answered that nature makes no one a slave.

Ahead of their time

Most Athenians took the gods, customs, and values of their city as given. Socrates kept asking what those ideas really meant, and in 399 BCE the city put him to death for it.

In most Greek cities, women were kept out of public life and learning. Epicurus welcomed women into his school. Most Greeks honoured the gods through public rites. Heraclitus mocked some of those rites openly.

What still echoes today

The word "democracy", the habit of asking for reasons, and the question of how to live a good life all come from these cities. So does a warning: Aristotle's claim that some people are slaves by nature was used, centuries later, to defend the enslavement of Native Americans.
