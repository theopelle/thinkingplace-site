Thinker

Niccolò Machiavelli

Florence, Italy · 1469 – 1527 · Humanists and Essayists

Keywords: The Prince, Discourses on Livy, virtù

Who he was

Machiavelli was born in Florence in 1469. From 1498 to 1512, while the Medici family was out of power, he served the Florentine Republic as a senior official in charge of diplomatic and military affairs. His missions let him watch rulers like Cesare Borgia build states up close.

He also raised a citizen militia, which took Pisa in 1509. But in 1512 the Medici returned and he lost his post. Wrongly suspected of plotting against them, he was jailed and tortured, then freed under an amnesty three weeks later.

He retired to his farm outside Florence and became a writer. There, around 1513, he wrote The Prince. It was published in 1532, five years after his death.

What he cared about

Machiavelli wanted to describe politics as it is, not as it should be. His themes:

  • Feared or loved: if a ruler cannot be both, fear is safer, but he must never be hated.
  • Appearances: most people judge by what they see; few know who you really are.
  • Act early: problems are easy to cure but hard to see at first, and easy to see but hard to cure later.
  • Change is hard: whoever brings a new order makes enemies of all who gained from the old one.
  • Virtù and fortune: a ruler's skill and daring matter, and so does fortune, which he must always reckon with.

What might surprise you

The man behind "Machiavellian" was a lifelong republican. His Discourses on Livy (around 1517) has been called a founding text of modern republicanism. Some 18th-century readers, Rousseau among them, even read The Prince as satire, though most scholars disagree.

Still, the book's hard edges are real. He admired Cesare Borgia and held that fraud, deceit, and violence can be necessary to keep power. In 1559 the Catholic Church put The Prince on its list of banned books.

Where to start reading

The Prince, chapters 15 to 18: the famous pages on how rulers should behave. W. K. Marriott's translation (1908) is free on Project Gutenberg.
