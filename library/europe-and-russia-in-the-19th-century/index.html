Era

Europe and Russia in the 19th century

about 1800–1900

Keywords: Industrial Revolution, 1848, serfdom, Victorian, Darwin

Factories, revolutions, and new science shook the old certainties, and thinkers asked what a person should live by once faith, rank, and tradition no longer held.

How people lived

In Britain, large numbers of people left the land for the factory towns. Before 1833, some cotton mills ran up to 71 hours a week, and children often started work at four or five. Crowded, dirty housing helped spread cholera and typhoid. Laws slowly capped working hours and banned young children from the mines.

In 1848, revolutions broke out in more than fifty countries. People demanded a voice in government, a free press, and rights for workers. Most of the uprisings were crushed, but some reforms lasted, such as the end of serfdom in Austria and Hungary.

In Russia, the tsar feared the revolutions would spread. In 1849 the young Dostoevsky was arrested for taking part in a circle that discussed banned books. He and the others were led out to be shot and pardoned only at the last moment, in a scene staged on the tsar's orders. He spent the next years in prison and exile.

What they believed

Most Europeans were still Christian, but faith was under pressure. In 1859 Darwin published On the Origin of Species, which argued that living things evolve through natural selection. It clashed with the belief that each species was designed and fixed.

In 1882 Nietzsche wrote that "God is dead". He did not mean a being had died. He meant that belief in the Christian God, which had long given Europe its morals and meaning, was fading, and that people had not yet faced what that would mean.

Who counted, and who was left out

In Russia, millions of peasants were serfs, bound to the land and to their masters. In 1861 the tsar freed more than 23 million of them. Most then had to pay for their land over 49 years.

Women had few legal rights, and no vote. Mary Ann Evans published her novels as George Eliot, in part so they would be judged on their merits and not as "women's writing".

Ahead of their time

The law gave husbands power over their wives, and most people saw this as the natural order. In 1869, in The Subjection of Women, Mill called it wrong and argued for full equality between the sexes. He even compared the position of married women under the law to slavery.

Living with a man outside marriage broke the rules of Victorian society. The writer George Henry Lewes could not divorce his wife under English law, so George Eliot lived with him openly, as his wife in all but name, from 1854 until his death in 1878. It shocked many, and her own brother cut her off. She refused to hide it.

What still echoes today

The great questions of this century were moral ones. If the old rules no longer hold, what should we live by? Dostoevsky and Tolstoy looked for an answer in faith and conscience, Mill in freedom and fairness, and Nietzsche in the courage to create new values.

We still live with their questions, in a world of fast change, fading traditions, and choices that are more and more our own.
