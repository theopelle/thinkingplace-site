Theme

Work and wealth

Keywords: money, poverty, riches, labour, possessions

The question in one line

How much is enough, and what is work for?

How the traditions see it

Epicurus and Marcus Aurelius (Epicureanism, Stoicism). Epicurus said nature's wealth is limited and easy to get, while the wealth of empty wishes has no end. Marcus Aurelius, emperor of Rome, reminded himself each morning that he was rising to do the work of a human being.

Confucius and Sa'di (Confucianism, Persian and Sufi Poetry). Confucius could find joy with coarse rice and water, and called riches gained by wrongdoing a floating cloud. Being poor without complaining is hard, he said; being rich without pride is easy. Sa'di advised anyone who wants riches to ask for contentment instead.

Adam Smith and Rousseau (Enlightenment Ethics). Adam Smith argued in The Wealth of Nations that wealth grows through free exchange and the division of labour, as if guided by an "invisible hand". Yet his first book began with sympathy: however selfish we are, we care about others' happiness. Rousseau saw a darker side: the first person who fenced off land, said "This is mine", and found people simple enough to believe it, opened the way to crimes, wars, and misery.

Vivekananda and Schopenhauer (Indian Philosophy, Existential Thought). Vivekananda taught that the best work is done for no reward, neither money nor fame. Schopenhauer compared riches to sea water: the more you drink, the thirstier you get.

Where they agree, and where they don't

Most of them warn that wealth beyond need rarely brings peace. They differ on what wealth does for society. Smith saw trade and work as the way whole nations grow richer; Rousseau saw property as the root of inequality; Marcus Aurelius and Vivekananda care less about the wealth than about the spirit in which we work.
