Theme

Ego, pride, and humility

Keywords: vanity, self-love, arrogance, modesty, self-conceit

The question in one line

How much should we think of ourselves?

How the traditions see it

Laozi (Taoism). The Tao Te Ching praises water: it helps everything and settles in the low places others avoid. A cup filled to the brim is hard to carry. Knowing others is clever, it says, but knowing yourself is wisdom.

Confucius (Confucianism). Confucius did not worry about being unknown, only about not knowing others. He noted that people of old studied to improve themselves, while people of his day studied to be seen. His students said he was free of four things, among them stubbornness and egoism.

Epictetus (Stoicism). The first task of anyone who studies philosophy, said Epictetus, is to drop self-conceit, because nobody can learn what they think they already know. He also asked his students to remember how small a part of the whole they are.

La Rochefoucauld and Erasmus (Humanists and Essayists). La Rochefoucauld called self-love the greatest of flatterers, and saw it behind even our virtues. In Erasmus's Praise of Folly, Folly herself admits that nothing is sillier than admiring yourself.

Where they agree, and where they don't

All of them see pride as a blind spot that keeps us from learning and from seeing others clearly. They differ on the cure. Laozi, Confucius, and Epictetus think practice can make us humbler. La Rochefoucauld doubts we ever fully escape self-love; at best we learn to notice it.
