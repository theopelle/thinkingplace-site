Era

Japan, from the Heian court to the age of the samurai

10th–14th century

Keywords: Heian, Kamakura, samurai, court women, mappō, medieval Japan

A refined court that prized beauty, a country slowly taken over by warriors, and a Buddhism that believed the world was in decline.

How people lived

In 794 the emperor moved his capital to Heian-kyō, today's Kyoto, and gave the age its name. Over time, real power passed to a great noble family, the Fujiwara. The court was tiny: perhaps 5,000 aristocrats in a land of some five million people. In that small world, beauty was seen as part of what made a person good.

Outside the capital, a new class of provincial warriors, the samurai, grew stronger. A civil war between two warrior clans ended in 1185, and the victor, Minamoto no Yoritomo, set up a military government at Kamakura. The court survived, but the warriors ruled. They held off two Mongol invasions, in 1274 and 1281, before their own regime fell in 1333.

What they believed

Many Japanese Buddhists believed they lived in mappō, the age of decline of the Buddha's teaching, which by their count began around 1052. This belief helped give rise to new schools of Buddhism in the Kamakura years, such as the Pure Land school of Hōnen and the school of Nichiren. Zen also took root, brought by Eisai and Dōgen.

Disaster was never far away. In Hōjōki, Chōmei describes a great fire in 1177, a whirlwind and a forced move of the capital in 1180, and a famine in which one monk counted 42,300 bodies in part of the capital in just two months.

Who counted, and who was left out

The literature of this age was written by and for a tiny elite, a few thousand people in a land of millions. Within it, women were mostly kept from Chinese learning, the prestige writing of men. But they wrote in Japanese, in a script called "women's hand", and much of the great writing in Japanese came from court women.

Ahead of their time

Writing in Japanese, Sei Shōnagon filled her book with her own opinions, likes, and dislikes, with unusual freedom.

People who turned away from the world usually went to live in a temple. Chōmei took Buddhist vows in 1204, but chose another path: he built a tiny hut in the hills outside the capital and lived there alone. In Hōjōki he set down his life in the hut, after the disasters he had seen.

What still echoes today

Kenkō's question still speaks to us: should we look at the moon only when it is full, or at flowers only in full bloom? These writers taught that things can be beautiful because they pass.
