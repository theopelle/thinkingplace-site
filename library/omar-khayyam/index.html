Thinker

Omar Khayyam

Nishapur, Iran · 1048 – 1131 · Persian and Sufi Poetry

Keywords: Rubáiyát

Who he was

Omar Khayyam was born in Nishapur, in north-east Persia, and lived under the Seljuk sultans, around the time of the First Crusade. He was first of all a scientist. In mathematics, he found a general way to solve cubic equations using geometry, centuries before Descartes.

In 1074 the sultan Malik-Shah called him to his service and asked him to set up an observatory in Isfahan. There Khayyam measured the length of the solar year with great precision and designed a new calendar, which is still the basis of the Persian calendar today.

What he cared about

The short four-line poems, or quatrains, attributed to him are full of questions, doubt, and a love of the present:

  • Time cannot be undone: the moving finger writes, and nothing will cancel a line.
  • We cannot see behind the veil: the great questions stay without answers.
  • Learned arguments go round in circles, and we come out by the same door we went in.
  • Enjoy the present: a book of verses, bread, wine, and good company.
  • Everything passes, sultans and their glory included.

What might surprise you

We are not sure he wrote the poems at all. No collection survives from his lifetime, and some scholars think the whole tradition of "Khayyam's quatrains" may have been attached to his name later.

In his own time, his doubts made religious leaders suspicious, and after he fell from favour at court he made the pilgrimage to Mecca, perhaps partly to prove his faith. In the West he became famous through Edward FitzGerald's free English version of 1859, which the Pre-Raphaelites came to admire.

Where to start reading

FitzGerald's Rubáiyát of Omar Khayyám, first edition (1859): 75 quatrains, read in ten minutes, and free on Project Gutenberg. Remember it is FitzGerald's poem as much as Khayyam's.
