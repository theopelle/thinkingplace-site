Theme

Duty

Keywords: moral law, good will, categorical imperative, obligation, respect, svadharma

The question in one line

Should we do what is right even when we don't feel like it, and even when it costs us?

How the traditions see it

Kant (Enlightenment Ethics). For Kant, nothing is good without condition except a good will. An act has moral worth only when it is done from duty, out of respect for the moral law, not from feeling or for the results. That law comes from reason itself, and it commands without conditions: Kant calls it the categorical imperative. Because every person has this reason, every person has equal worth and deserves equal respect.

The Stoics (Stoicism). The Stoics spoke of kathekon, the fitting or proper action: what our nature and our roles call for, as parents, citizens, or friends. Cicero's On Duties, one of the most read books of Roman ethics, is built on this idea. Marcus Aurelius put it shortly: stop talking about what a good person should be, and be one.

Confucius and Mencius (Confucianism). In Confucian ethics, duty lives in relationships: to parents, rulers, friends, and strangers. Righteousness, yi, became one of the five constant virtues of later Confucianism, beside benevolence, ritual, wisdom, and trustworthiness. Mencius said a person should simply not do what their own sense of righteousness forbids.

The Bhagavad Gita (Indian Philosophy). The Gita speaks of svadharma, your own duty, set by your nature and place in life. Arjuna must fight, because that is his duty as a warrior, but without craving the fruits of his action. (See also Karma and dharma.)

Where they agree, and where they don't

All of them say duty is not just a feeling we wait for. They disagree on where it comes from. For Kant, it comes from reason, which every person shares. For the Stoics and Confucius, it comes from our nature and our roles. For the Gita, it comes from our station, which in its world was tied to birth. Mill, by contrast, saw the inner pull of duty as a feeling in our own mind, trained by upbringing. And in 1943 Simone Weil argued that duties come before rights: a right means something only when others are bound to respect it.
