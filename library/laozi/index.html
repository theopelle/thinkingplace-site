Thinker

Laozi

China · traditionally 6th century BCE · Taoism

Keywords: Tao Te Ching, wu wei, the Way

Who he was

We are not sure. The earliest accounts say Laozi, "the Old Master", was a man called Li Er or Lao Dan, who kept the archives at the Zhou court and was an older contemporary of Confucius. Scholars are divided: some accept that a Lao Dan probably lived, others see him as largely or wholly a legend.

The best-known story says that, at the age of 80, he set off west to live as a hermit. At the last gate, a guard named Yinxi recognised him and asked him to write down his wisdom before leaving. What he wrote, the story goes, was the Tao Te Ching.

What he cared about

The Tao Te Ching has 81 short chapters, many only a few lines long. Its main ideas:

  • The Tao: the way of all things, which cannot be fully named.
  • Effortless action: the wise act without forcing, and things get done.
  • Water as a model: soft and low, it serves everything and wears down the hardest stone.
  • Less is more: fewer desires, fewer possessions, fewer rules.
  • Quiet leadership: the best rulers are barely noticed.

What might surprise you

The oldest pieces of the Tao Te Ching ever found were written on bamboo strips buried around 300 BCE. They hold only about 40 per cent of the text we read today, which suggests the book grew over time.

Later, Laozi became far more than a writer. From the Han dynasty on, religious Taoism worshipped him as a god, and the Tang emperors claimed him as their ancestor.

Where to start reading

The Tao Te Ching: 81 short chapters, best read one at a time. James Legge's 1891 translation is free on Project Gutenberg.
