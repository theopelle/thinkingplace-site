Path

Epicureanism

Keywords: ataraxia, pleasure, the Garden, atoms, friendship

A philosophy of quiet happiness: simple pleasures, good friends, and a mind free from fear.

Where and when

Epicurus founded the school in Athens around 306 BCE. It met in his garden, so it became known simply as "the Garden", and it welcomed women among its students. In the 1st century BCE, the Roman poet Lucretius set out its ideas in a long poem, On the Nature of Things.

The school was a rival first of Plato's followers, then of the Stoics. It declined as Christianity spread; Dante even placed the Epicureans in hell. Later thinkers rediscovered it, and Thomas Jefferson called himself an Epicurean.

Key ideas

  • Pleasure is the goal of life, but the pleasure that counts is the absence of pain in the body and of fear in the mind.
  • Desire less. Some desires are natural and easy to meet; others, like the hunger for wealth and power, can never be satisfied.
  • Death is nothing to fear: when it is here, we are not.
  • The world is made of atoms and empty space. The gods, if they exist, do not interfere in human life.
  • Friendship is the greatest help to a happy life.

Next to its neighbours

The word "epicurean" now suggests fine food and luxury. That is close to the opposite of what Epicurus taught: he and his friends were known for simple meals and long conversations. Next to the Stoics, the Epicureans also sought a calm mind, but through pleasure rather than virtue, and by keeping out of politics rather than serving the city.
