Two paths

Classical Greek Philosophy and Enlightenment Ethics

Classical Greek Philosophy · Enlightenment Ethics

Aristotle asks what kind of person to be; the Enlightenment adds two more questions, what is my duty and what brings the most happiness: between them, three of the main ways people still judge right and wrong.

Where they agree

  • Reason should lead. Aristotle calls the law "reason unaffected by desire"; Kant grounds duty in reason, binding on every rational being.
  • Test ideas in argument. Socrates said the unexamined life is not worth living; Mill, that whoever knows only their own side of a case knows little even of that.

Where they part ways

  • What makes an act right. For Aristotle, what a person of good character and practical wisdom would do. For Kant, the will behind it: only a good will is good without condition. For Mill, its results: the most happiness.
  • Happiness and morality. Aristotle calls happiness the one thing we choose for its own sake. Kant says morality is not about making ourselves happy, but about becoming worthy of happiness.
  • Who counts. Aristotle argued that some people are slaves by nature. The Enlightenment spoke of freedom for all and of women's minds, while many of its colonies still ran on slave labour.
