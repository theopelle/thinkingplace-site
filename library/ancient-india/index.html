Era

Ancient India

about 7th century BCE–2nd century CE

Keywords: Vedas, Buddha, shramanas, caste, Indo-Greeks

An age of forest teachers, wandering monks, and royal debates, when India asked who we really are beneath our roles, and how to act well in a world of endless rebirth.

A word on dates

Dates in this era are uncertain, often by centuries. These texts were spoken and memorised long before they were written down, and many grew over time. Scholars place the oldest Upanishads around the 7th to 6th century BCE, and the Bhagavad Gita anywhere from the 2nd century BCE to the 2nd century CE. We give ranges, not exact years.

How people lived

In the Ganges plain, kingdoms grew, and kings held court with learned priests. One famous Upanishad describes a contest of ten sages at the court of King Janaka, debating the nature of reality before the king.

Alongside the priests lived the shramanas, people who left home and family to seek truth as wandering ascetics. Several such movements already existed by the 6th century BCE. The Buddha and the Jain teacher Mahavira came from this world in the 5th century BCE.

What they believed

The Vedas, old hymns and rituals kept by priests, were the core of religious life. The Upanishads turned inward. They taught that Atman, the inner self, and Brahman, the reality behind everything, are one and the same.

Ideas of karma and rebirth took shape in this period, perhaps first among the ascetics. Many shramana groups rejected the authority of the Vedas and their rituals.

Who counted, and who was left out

An old hymn describes society in four classes: priests, rulers and warriors, farmers and merchants, and labourers and servants. Scholars see this, in early times, more as an ideal than a fixed reality. People outside the four classes were treated as outcastes.

Women appear in the debates, if rarely. Gargi questions the sage Yajnavalkya at Janaka's court, and his wife Maitreyi asks him what makes a person immortal. They are the exception in texts written and kept by men.

Ahead of their time

In a society organised by class, many held that birth is what makes a person a priest. Early Buddhist texts disagreed. In one dialogue, a priest is led to agree that what truly counts is virtue and wisdom, not birth.

Many seekers of the time believed the spiritual life meant leaving home and family behind. The Bhagavad Gita offered another way. Krishna tells the warrior Arjuna to do his duty in the world, but without craving the results. Action itself can be a spiritual path.

What still echoes today

In the 2nd century BCE, Indo-Greek kings ruled in the north-west. The Questions of King Milinda records, or imagines, a long dialogue between one of them, Menander, and the Buddhist monk Nagasena. It is an early meeting of two worlds, built on patient questions.

The Gita's advice to act fully while letting go of the outcome still speaks to anyone who works hard for results they cannot control.
