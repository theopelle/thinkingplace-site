Thinker

René Descartes

La Haye (now Descartes, France) · 1596 – 1650 · Enlightenment Ethics

Keywords: Discourse on Method

Who he was

Descartes was born in 1596 in Touraine, in France. His mother died when he was a year old. He was a sickly child, studied with the Jesuits at La Flèche, and took a law degree to please his father.

Instead of practising law, he joined the Dutch army as a volunteer to learn military engineering. One cold night in 1619, shut in a room heated by a stove, he had three dreams that he believed revealed a new philosophy to him.

He spent more than 20 years in the Dutch Republic and wrote all his major works there. In 1649 Queen Christina of Sweden invited him to her court. He died in Stockholm the next winter, probably of pneumonia.

What he cared about

Descartes wanted knowledge built on foundations so firm that nothing could shake them. His themes:

  • Doubt as a method: accept nothing as true until you see clearly that it is.
  • I think, therefore I am: even when doubting everything, he could not doubt that he was thinking.
  • Good sense, well used: everyone has reason; what matters is how we direct it.
  • Master yourself: change your desires rather than the order of the world.
  • Reading as conversation: good books let us talk with the best minds of past ages.

What might surprise you

He was a great mathematician as well as a philosopher. He joined algebra and geometry, and the graph grid with x and y axes, the Cartesian coordinate system, is named after him. When Galileo was condemned in 1633, Descartes put his own book on the physical world in a drawer.

His private life was more ordinary than his reputation. With a servant, Helena Jans van der Strom, he had a daughter, Francine, who died of scarlet fever at five. In Sweden he was asked to teach the queen at 5 a.m. in a cold castle; they met only a few times and did not like each other.

Where to start reading

Discourse on Method: short, personal, and readable in an afternoon. John Veitch's translation (1850) is free on Project Gutenberg.
