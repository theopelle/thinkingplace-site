Theme

Work and action

Keywords: effort, doing, karma yoga, wu wei, labour

The question in one line

How should we act, and what should we expect from it?

How the traditions see it

The Bhagavad Gita and Vivekananda (Indian Philosophy). In the Gita, Krishna tells Arjuna that his business is with the action alone, never with its fruits. It is better to do your own task imperfectly than someone else's well. Vivekananda put it simply: work without stopping, but without attachment.

Marcus Aurelius (Stoicism). Even an emperor found it hard to get up in the morning. His reminder to himself: I am rising to do the work of a human being. He also told himself to stop talking about what a good man should be, and simply be one.

Laozi and Zhuangzi (Taoism). The Taoists value action that does not force. Governing a great state, says the Tao Te Ching, is like cooking small fish: stir too much and they fall apart. Zhuangzi's cook works with the grain of things, and his knife stays sharp for nineteen years.

Confucius and Voltaire (Confucianism, Enlightenment Ethics). Confucius praised the person who acts first and speaks afterwards. At the end of Voltaire's Candide, after war, shipwreck, and earthquake, the hero stops arguing about whether all is for the best. "We must cultivate our garden."

Where they agree, and where they don't

All of them trust deeds over words. They differ on effort and reward. The Gita and the Stoics speak of duty done well whatever the result; the Taoists warn against forcing things; Voltaire suggests that useful work close to home may be the best answer to questions we cannot solve.
