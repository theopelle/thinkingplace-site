Thinker

Kenkō

Japan · about 1283 – 1350 · Japanese Aesthetics

Keywords: Essays in Idleness

Who he was

Kenkō was the son of an official and served as an officer of the guards at the imperial palace in Kyoto. Later in life he left public life and became a Buddhist monk and hermit.

Nobody knows why. One story says it was an unhappy love; another, grief at the death of a former emperor. He kept writing poetry and took part in poetry contests at court, but his fame rests on one book, written between 1330 and 1332.

What he cared about

Essays in Idleness is a collection of 243 short pieces, from a single line to a few pages. He wrote in a "follow the brush" style, letting one thought lead to the next. His main themes:

  • Impermanence: everything passes, and that is part of its beauty. Japanese tradition calls this feeling mono no aware.
  • Death: it comes sooner than we think, so do not waste your days.
  • Nature and small things: flowers before they bloom, a garden strewn with fallen ones, the moon as it wanes.
  • People: friendship, manners, and the follies of those around him.

What might surprise you

For a monk writing about death, Kenkō is often funny. He tells little stories from everyday life, like the archery teacher who tells a beginner to carry only one arrow, and he has firm views on taste: a little imperfection, he says, beats perfect sameness.

The book was not published in his lifetime and was not even divided into numbered passages until the 17th century. Even his name has a twist: "Yoshida Kenkō" comes from later documents that recent research has shown to be forged.

Where to start reading

Essays in Idleness: open it anywhere and read one passage at a time. William N. Porter's 1914 translation, A Miscellany of a Japanese Priest, is free on archive.org.
