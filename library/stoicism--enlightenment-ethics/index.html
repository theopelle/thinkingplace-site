Stoicism and Enlightenment Ethics

Stoicism · Enlightenment Ethics

Several Enlightenment thinkers came close to the Stoics on mastering our desires through reason; they part on whether the passions are to be corrected or are the very force that moves us.

Where they agree

  • Change yourself, not the world. Descartes resolved to conquer himself rather than fortune, and to change his desires rather than the order of the world; Epictetus divides things into those in our power and those that are not.
  • Understanding frees us. Spinoza says an emotion stops being a passion once we understand it clearly; Marcus Aurelius says it is our judgment about a thing, not the thing, that pains us.
  • Happiness should depend on us. Hume advises placing our happiness chiefly on things that depend on ourselves, as the Stoics did.

Where they part ways

  • Reason and the passions. For the Stoics, strong emotions come from false judgments, and reason can correct them. Hume turns this around: reason, he says, serves the passions and can never rule them.
  • A reasonable universe? The Stoics trusted that the universe itself is one ordered, living whole, so living by nature means living by reason. The Enlightenment asked for evidence first: for Hume, a wise person proportions belief to the evidence.
