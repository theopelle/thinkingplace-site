Thinker

Jean-Jacques Rousseau

Geneva, Switzerland · 1712 – 1778 · Enlightenment Ethics

Keywords: Emile, Discourse on Inequality, Reveries of the Solitary Walker

Who he was

Rousseau was born in Geneva in 1712, the son of a watchmaker. His mother died nine days after his birth. Apprenticed to an engraver who beat him, he left Geneva as a teenager and drifted through Savoy and France.

In Paris he became a friend of Diderot and wrote for the Encyclopédie. Fame came suddenly in 1750, when his essay arguing that the arts and sciences had corrupted naturally good people won a prize. He also wrote music, and his opera The Village Soothsayer pleased King Louis XV.

In 1762 he published The Social Contract and Emile. Both were condemned: his books were burned and warrants issued for his arrest. He spent years moving from refuge to refuge, grew fearful of plots against him, and died at Ermenonville in 1778.

What he cared about

Rousseau asked why people born good and free end up unequal and unhappy. His themes:

  • Freedom lost in society: people are born free, yet everywhere live in chains of their own making.
  • Inequality: private property, he argued, is where social inequality begins.
  • Citizens, not spectators: a free people takes part in its shared affairs; once citizens stop caring, the state is lost.
  • Feeling, not just years: the one who has lived most is the one who has most felt life.
  • Vanity: much unhappiness comes from caring only about our standing against others.

What might surprise you

The great writer on education gave away his own children. By his own account, he and Thérèse Levasseur, later his wife, had five children, and all were left at a foundling hospital soon after birth, then almost a death sentence. Later in life he said he regretted it.

His picture of the family also rested on women serving men. In Emile he argued that girls should be educated to please men, which is why Mary Wollstonecraft attacked him by name. His later books, the Confessions and the Reveries of the Solitary Walker, turn inward, looking closely at his own feelings and memories.

Where to start reading

The Social Contract, Book I: short chapters, and the famous opening line. Free on Project Gutenberg in English; the app uses its own translations from the French.
