Era

The Enlightenment

18th century

Keywords: Age of Reason, 18th century, philosophes, salons, Encyclopédie, slave trade

The century that told people to think for themselves and put reason, rights, and freedom at the centre of public life, while leaving out much of humanity.

How people lived

Most Europeans still lived under kings. Some rulers, such as Frederick the Great of Prussia and Catherine the Great of Russia, took up new ideas and called themselves enlightened. Ideas travelled through salons, coffeehouses, scientific academies, and a fast-growing world of print.

In Paris, elite women such as Madame Geoffrin hosted salons where writers met to argue. From 1751 to 1772, Diderot and d'Alembert, with about 150 others, published the Encyclopédie, 28 volumes of text and plates meant to gather all human knowledge.

What they believed

Christianity was still the official faith almost everywhere, but many writers questioned the power of churches. Their faith was in reason and in evidence. Kant summed up the spirit of the age in two Latin words, sapere aude: dare to know.

Thinking freely had a price. In France, writers fought the censors and were sometimes jailed or forced into exile. Voltaire spent time in the Bastille twice, and one of his books was publicly burned. Many banned books were printed in the Dutch Republic and smuggled into France.

Who counted, and who was left out

The age of rights was also an age of the Atlantic slave trade. Over the centuries of that trade, between 10 and 12.5 million Africans were forced onto ships to the Americas, and up to two million died on the crossing. Many countries that embraced the new ideas excluded slavery from them.

Women were rarely treated as equals in these debates. Émilie du Châtelet was once thrown out of a Paris café where scholars met, and came back dressed as a man.

Ahead of their time

Women could attend the Academy of Sciences in Paris only as listeners. Yet in 1739, Émilie du Châtelet's essay on fire became the first work by a woman the Academy published. Her French translation of Newton, finished in 1749, the year she died, is still the standard one.

Many writers, Rousseau among them, held that women should obey men. Mary Wollstonecraft disagreed. In 1792, in A Vindication of the Rights of Woman, she argued that women, given a real education, would be thinking people and better citizens, and should have civil and political rights of their own.

What still echoes today

The ideas of the age helped inspire the American and French Revolutions. In 1791, enslaved people in Haiti rose up. Their revolt forced revolutionary France to abolish slavery in 1794, and showed that the rights of man had to be claimed by those left out of them.

Human rights, free speech, and a free press are still argued in the language this century created. So is the question of who gets left out of "everyone".
