Theme

Law and rights

Keywords: human rights, natural rights, authority, rule of law, social contract

The question in one line

Where do laws get their authority, and what rights do we have?

How the traditions see it

Aristotle (Classical Greek Philosophy). Is it better to be ruled by the best person or the best laws? Aristotle leaned towards laws, because even good rulers have passions that bend their judgement. The law, he said, is reason unaffected by desire. Without law and justice, the human being is the worst of animals.

Confucius (Confucianism). Confucius trusted example more than rules. To govern means to correct, and a ruler who acts rightly is followed without giving orders. When wronged, repay injury with justice, and kindness with kindness.

Epicurus (Epicureanism). Epicurus saw no justice written into nature. Justice is an agreement between people not to harm each other or be harmed, and it can differ from place to place and time to time.

Locke, Rousseau, and Wollstonecraft (Enlightenment Ethics). Locke argued that all people have natural rights to life, liberty, and property, and that good law does not shrink freedom but protects and enlarges it. Rousseau wanted laws the people make together, and warned that a state is lost once citizens stop caring. Wollstonecraft demanded that rights include women.

Where they agree, and where they don't

Most of them see good law as a defence against the passions of the powerful; Sa'di put it simply: a tyrant cannot govern, for no one expects the shepherd's duty from the wolf. They split on where law comes from. For Aristotle and Locke, it rests on reason and nature; for Epicurus, on agreement; for Confucius, it matters less than the ruler's character.
