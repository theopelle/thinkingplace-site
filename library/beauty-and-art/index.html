Theme

Beauty and art

Keywords: aesthetics, taste, wabi-sabi, imperfection, the beautiful

The question in one line

Is beauty out there, or in the eye of the beholder?

How the traditions see it

Kenkō and Okakura (Japanese Aesthetics). The Japanese writers love what is unfinished. Kenkō thought it better to leave a little imperfection, since perfect uniformity is dull. In The Book of Tea, Okakura says true beauty is found by the one who completes the incomplete in their own mind: what is left unsaid gives the viewer a part to play.

Hume (Enlightenment Ethics). Hume started from the view that beauty is no quality in things, but exists in the mind that looks at them. Yet he did not leave it there. Some judgements are better than others, he argued, and the standard of taste is the shared verdict of rare, experienced, unprejudiced critics. Studying beauty, he added, softens the temper.

Laozi (Taoism). The Tao Te Ching sees a hidden cost in naming beauty: as soon as everyone knows what is beautiful, they also know what is ugly. Each idea brings its opposite with it.

Nietzsche and Emerson (Existential Thought, Humanists and Essayists). For Nietzsche, the greatest art is to "give style" to your own character, shaping strengths and weaknesses into one whole. Emerson saw in every work of genius our own rejected thoughts, coming back to us with a kind of borrowed majesty.

Where they agree, and where they don't

All of them link beauty to the person who sees it, not only to the object. They differ on how far. Hume searches for a standard that lets us say some tastes are better; the Japanese writers make the viewer a partner in the work; Nietzsche turns the art inward, onto how we shape our lives.
