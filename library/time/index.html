In their time

Where history meets philosophy

Before you readWhy it helps to know the world a thinker lived in.

At school, I had a history teacher who began the school year in an unusual way. In the first lesson, he taught no dates, no kings, and no battles. For two hours, he talked instead about how people of the past saw the world: what they feared, what they took for granted, what they could not even imagine. Historians call this the history of mentalities. His point was simple. You cannot understand what people did until you understand the world they did it in.

The same is true of ideas. A sentence written two thousand years ago can feel as if it was written for you this morning. Some ideas really are like that: Seneca's warning that we waste our lives, not that life is short, needs no footnote. Others only show their full weight once you know what surrounded them.

When Epicurus opened his school in Athens, he welcomed women as students, in a city where women were kept out of public life. Today that sounds ordinary; then, it was a quiet revolution. When Socrates kept asking Athenians what they really meant by justice or courage, he was not playing a game. In 399 BCE, a jury sentenced him to death.

It works the other way too. Aristotle, one of the sharpest minds who ever lived, argued that some people are slaves by nature. To him, that was common sense. To us, it is plainly wrong. Knowing his world does not excuse it, but it helps us see how a brilliant person can share the blind spots of his time, and it invites a harder question: which of our own certainties will look like that one day?

Some ideas changed our world precisely because they shocked theirs. Mary Wollstonecraft's claim that women seem weaker only because they are denied an education was radical in 1792. The fact that it sounds obvious now is, in part, her doing.

So as you read the thinkers in this Library, try asking two questions. What did this mean to the people who first heard it? And what does it mean to me, here, today? "In their time" is here to help with the first. The second is yours.

About the voices you'll find here

The Thinking Place draws on more than two thousand years of writing. For most of that time, few women were allowed to study, publish, or be translated, so most of the texts that survive, and that are free to share today, were written by men. We have included women wherever we could, from Sei Shōnagon at the Japanese court to Mary Wollstonecraft, Émilie du Châtelet, and Simone Weil, and we will keep adding more as we find good translations.
