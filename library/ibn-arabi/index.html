Thinker

Ibn Arabi

Murcia, Spain · 1165 – 1240 · Persian and Sufi Poetry

Keywords: Tarjuman al-Ashwaq

Who he was

Ibn Arabi was born in 1165 in Murcia, in Muslim Spain, and grew up in Seville, where his family served the ruler. As a boy he preferred military games to books. Then, as a teenager, he had a vision that turned him towards the Sufi path.

His father mentioned the change to the great philosopher Averroes, who asked to meet him. Ibn Arabi said the meeting taught him the difference between knowledge gained by reasoning and insight that comes by unveiling.

At 33 he left Spain for good. He travelled for 25 years, through North Africa, Mecca, Baghdad, and Anatolia, then settled in Damascus in 1223 to teach and write. He died there in 1240.

What he cared about

Ibn Arabi was a mystic, philosopher, and poet who wrote in Arabic, not Persian: he belongs to this path as a Sufi master whose ideas spread across the Islamic world. About 800 works are attributed to him. His themes:

  • The unity of being: everything that exists is a face of one single reality, God.
  • A heart open to every form: a true lover can find the divine in a monastery, a temple, or the Kaaba.
  • Love as a religion: he follows love wherever it leads.
  • Imagination: not a trick of the mind but a real way of knowing the world.
  • The complete human: a person who joins the divine and the created world in themselves.

What might surprise you

His love poems are also theology. The Interpreter of Desires reads like a collection of classic Arabic love odes, yet he wrote a commentary explaining them as mystical poems. The famous lines on a heart that can hold every form come from it.

He was, and still is, contested. Many jurists saw him as a heretic, while Sufis called him "the Greatest Master" and some Muslims revere him as a saint. His ideas spread quickly across the Islamic world after his death.

Where to start reading

The Interpreter of Desires (Tarjumán al-Ashwáq): short odes with his own notes. R. A. Nicholson's 1911 translation is free on Wikisource.
