Path

Taoism

Keywords: Tao, wu wei, the Way, water, softness, emptiness

A Chinese tradition of living in harmony with the Tao, the natural way of things, through simplicity and effortless action.

Where and when

Taoist thought took shape in China during the Warring States period, between about 450 and 300 BCE. Its two founding texts began to take shape then, and kept growing for some time after: the Tao Te Ching, short poetic chapters traditionally credited to Laozi, and the Zhuangzi, full of stories.

Tradition makes Laozi an archivist at the Zhou court and an older contemporary of Confucius; scholars are divided, and some think he is partly or wholly legendary. Taoism later also became an organised religion, with its own priests, rituals, and scriptures. The Tao Te Ching is one of the most translated books in the world, second only to the Bible.

Key ideas

  • The Tao is the way of things: always changing, and impossible to fully put into words.
  • Effortless action (wu wei): act without forcing, like water finding its way around a stone.
  • Naturalness (ziran): let things be what they are.
  • Three treasures: compassion, humility, and frugality.
  • Softness beats hardness: the yielding outlasts the rigid.

Who we read

  • Laozitraditionally 6th century BCE
  • Zhuangziabout 369 – 286 BCE

Next to its neighbours

Taoism and Confucianism grew up side by side and are often read as a pair. Confucians build a good society through learning, ritual, and duty. Taoists distrust that effort and prefer to step back: fewer rules, less striving, more trust in nature. Both shaped Chinese life for over two thousand years, and many people drew on both.
