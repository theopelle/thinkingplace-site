Era

America in the 19th century

about 1830–1900

Keywords: United States, Transcendentalism, slavery, abolition, Civil War, Seneca Falls

A young country that preached freedom while holding millions in slavery, where a small circle of New England writers told people to trust their own conscience over custom and the crowd.

How people lived

The United States grew fast and pushed west. Under the Indian Removal Act of 1830, somewhere between 60,000 and 100,000 Native Americans were forced from their lands in the south-east. About 15,000 of them died on the way, on what became known as the Trail of Tears.

In 1860 about four million people were enslaved, nearly one American in eight. The country went to war with itself from 1861 to 1865. Slavery was abolished by the Thirteenth Amendment in December 1865.

What they believed

Most Americans were Protestant Christians. In 1836, near Boston, Emerson and a few friends began meeting to discuss what they found lacking in the churches and colleges of their day. Critics called them "Transcendentalists", and the name stuck.

Emerson's essay Nature, published that same year, became their manifesto. Their message, in short: look for truth in your own experience and in nature, not only in books and pulpits.

Who counted, and who was left out

Women could not vote, and few had a public voice.

In 1848, about 300 people met at Seneca Falls, New York, for the first women's rights convention. The call for women's right to vote was the most debated resolution. Frederick Douglass, the only African American present, spoke in favour.

Ahead of their time

The law of the land allowed slavery and backed a war of conquest against Mexico. Thoreau argued that a person's conscience can outrank the law. In 1846 he refused to pay a tax that might help fund a war he opposed and a system that allowed slavery, and spent a night in jail. His essay on it, now known as Civil Disobedience, later inspired Gandhi and Martin Luther King Jr.

Women were expected to keep to the home. Margaret Fuller edited the Transcendentalists' journal, The Dial, and in 1845 published Woman in the Nineteenth Century, which urged women to seek independence through education and work. It is often called the first major feminist book in the United States.

What still echoes today

These writers asked how a person keeps their integrity inside a society they disagree with. Thoreau's answer was quiet refusal. Fuller's was to speak up in public.

Emily Dickinson's answer was a private world of words. She wrote nearly 1,800 poems, but only about ten were published in her lifetime, altered to fit the taste of the day. The rest were collected only after her death, from 1890.

Their ideas about self-reliance, nature, and conscience shaped American culture, and the question they raised is still with us: when should you follow the rules, and when should you follow your own judgement?
