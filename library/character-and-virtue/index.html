Theme

Character and virtue

Keywords: integrity, junzi, superior man, moral character, honesty

The question in one line

What makes a person good, and can you become one?

How the traditions see it

Aristotle (Classical Greek Philosophy). Virtue, for Aristotle, is a settled trait of character, and you build it the way you learn a craft: by doing. We become just by doing just acts and brave by doing brave ones. Each virtue sits between two extremes, like courage between cowardice and recklessness. Hitting that middle point is hard, and it takes practical wisdom.

Confucius (Confucianism). People start out much alike, Confucius said, and practice makes them grow far apart. The ideal is the "superior man", who asks a lot of himself and little of others, and who leads by example rather than by orders. Virtue is never lonely: whoever practises it will find neighbours.

The Dhammapada (Buddhist Philosophy). The Dhammapada puts the work in each person's hands: by oneself evil is done, and by oneself it is left undone. Small good acts add up, like a water jar filling drop by drop. Anger is overcome by love, and evil by good.

Kant (Enlightenment Ethics). Kant moved the focus from character to the will. Talents, courage, and even happiness can be used for bad ends; only a good will is good without qualification. Morality, he said, is not about how to be happy, but how to become worthy of happiness.

Where they agree, and where they don't

All four say goodness takes effort and is not simply given at birth. They split on where it lives. For Aristotle and Confucius, it is a character shaped by long practice and good company; for Kant, it is the intention behind each act, done out of duty. Virtue ethics, the tradition of Aristotle and Confucius, faded in the West after the Enlightenment and returned in the late 1950s.
