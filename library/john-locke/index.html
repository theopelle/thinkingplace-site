Thinker

John Locke

Wrington, England · 1632 – 1704 · Enlightenment Ethics

Keywords: An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Second Treatise of Government

Who he was

Locke was born in 1632 to Puritan parents in Somerset, England. He grew up through the civil war, the execution of King Charles I, and the return of the monarchy. He studied at Oxford and stayed 15 years, drawn more to medicine and experiment than to the old curriculum.

In 1666 a chance meeting brought him into the household of Lord Shaftesbury, a leading politician. As his doctor, Locke oversaw a risky operation that Shaftesbury credited with saving his life. When Shaftesbury fell from favour, Locke fled to the Dutch Republic in 1683.

He came home after the Glorious Revolution of 1688 and published his major works within a year or two. He spent his last 14 years in the country house of his friend, the philosopher Lady Masham, and died there in 1704.

What he cared about

Locke asked where our knowledge comes from and what makes a government legitimate. His themes:

  • The mind as a blank slate: we are born without ready-made ideas; everything we know comes from experience.
  • Government by consent: rulers hold power only with the people's consent, and a tyrant who loses it may be resisted.
  • Law protects freedom: good law does not shrink our liberty; it preserves and enlarges it.
  • Toleration: faith is a personal matter, and churches should not force belief.
  • New ideas deserve a hearing: a truth is no less true for being new.

What might surprise you

The "father of liberalism" was tied to the slave trade. He held shares in a company that traded enslaved Africans, and he helped draft the Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina, which gave colonists absolute power over their slaves. Historians still debate how far he shared those views.

He also kept his name off his own revolution. Two Treatises of Government and A Letter Concerning Toleration came out anonymously. Their ideas later shaped the language of the American Declaration of Independence.

Where to start reading

Second Treatise of Government, chapters 2 to 9: the core of his case for freedom and consent. Free on Project Gutenberg.
