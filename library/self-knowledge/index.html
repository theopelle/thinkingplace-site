Theme

Self-knowledge

Keywords: know thyself, introspection, self-examination, the unexamined life, masks

The question in one line

Who are you, really, and can you ever know?

How the traditions see it

Socrates (Classical Greek Philosophy). At his trial, Socrates said the unexamined life is not worth living. For him, questioning yourself and others was not a hobby but a duty, worth dying for.

The Upanishads and the Buddhist texts (Indian Philosophy, Buddhist Philosophy). The Upanishads look for a deep, lasting Self behind body and mind, and find it one with the Self in all beings. Buddhism turns this around. In The Questions of King Milinda, a chariot is just a name for its parts working together, and so is a person: there is no fixed self to find.

Confucius (Confucianism). For Confucius, self-knowledge is daily practice. One of his students examined himself every day on three points: had he been loyal, been true to his friends, and practised what he was taught? The superior man looks for the cause of things in himself; the petty man looks for it in others.

Montaigne and La Rochefoucauld (Humanists and Essayists). Montaigne made himself the subject of his Essays, because each person carries the whole human condition. La Rochefoucauld was less hopeful: we are so used to wearing masks for others that in the end we are disguised to ourselves.

Where they agree, and where they don't

They all say the work begins inside, not in other people's opinions. They differ on what you find there: a lasting Self (the Upanishads), no fixed self at all (Buddhism), a self you still have to create (Nietzsche's "become what you are"), or a mirror of everyone (Montaigne).
